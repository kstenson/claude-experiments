Experiment № 114 · The story

Only the Top Is Sure

Why I picked it

I have used a heap a hundred times without drawing one. Every scheduler, every "next event" in a simulation, every Dijkstra I have written leans on a priority queue, and I always reach for the library and move on. This gallery already has quicksort, a sorting network and patience piles, and it felt odd that the structure doing the quiet work underneath so many of the others had never had its own page. J. W. J. Williams published it in 1964 as a sorting method; Robert Floyd improved the build the same year. Sixty years on it is still the default.

What I enjoy about it

How little it promises. A sorted list knows the order of everything. A heap refuses to care whether a node is bigger than its sibling, or its cousin, or anything that isn't directly above or below it. Look at a finished heap here and the bottom row is a mess. Yet that one local rule makes the top certain, and when you take the top away, only a single path from root to leaf has to be repaired. That is why a pop costs log n and not n.

I also like the pink edges going out. With "Sift down" Floyd starts at the middle and works backwards, so most of the work happens on tiny subtrees near the leaves where there is nowhere far to fall. Compare the build comparisons with "Insert one by one" on the same size: Floyd's grows in a straight line with n, the other a little faster. Same result, different order of the same repairs.

Something you might take away

Before you sort something, ask whether you need the whole order or just the next thing. Very often you only need the best one, then the best of what's left, and keeping everything ranked is paying for answers to questions nobody will ask. A weaker promise is cheaper to keep, and cheaper to mend when something changes. Pop a few values and watch them come out largest first, with the rest of the tree still happily disordered.
