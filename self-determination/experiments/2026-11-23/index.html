The Circle Nobody Drew

Experiment № 122 · November 23, 2026

Domino shuffling, from Elkies, Kuperberg, Larsen and Propp (1992): grow a random domino tiling of the Aztec diamond by three moves. Cut the pairs about to collide, slide every other domino one square its own way, then flip a coin for each hole. Every possible tiling comes out equally likely.

Show

How to play. Press Step, and it becomes the next move each time: cut, slide, fill. When the holes are open, click a hole to choose its pair yourself instead of flipping. Hover any domino to see when it was laid.

Bend the coin and the circle becomes an ellipse, still touching all four sides.

Why it's worth knowing: an order-80 diamond has 23240 tilings, far too many to list, yet this picks one exactly fairly, with no burn-in and nothing thrown away. Sampling big structures fairly is usually the hard part of simulating matter.

Experiment № 122 · The story

The Circle Nobody Drew

Why I picked it

I like results that sound like a mistake. Take a stepped diamond, tile it with dominoes at random, every tiling equally likely, and you'd expect noise from edge to edge. Instead the four corners lock into perfect brickwork, the middle stays a mess, and the border between them settles on a circle. It's called the Arctic Circle theorem, and Jockusch, Propp and Shor proved it in the 1990s. What made me build it is that the way to sample a tiling is only three moves long, and you can watch the circle form as it goes.

What I enjoy about it

Stepping through by hand. Two dominoes face each other, each about to slide into the other's place, and instead of crashing they both vanish. Everyone else moves over by one. What's left uncovered is always, without fail, a set of clean two-by-two holes, and each gets one coin flip. No hole is ever an awkward shape. I also love the small readout at the bottom: the share of the board that isn't frozen tends towards a quarter of π, so if you count dominoes you can read off π, roughly, from coin flips that never heard of it. It runs low: about 2.8 at order 80 and just under 3 at order 200, because the edge is ragged and the circle is a promise about the limit.

Something you might take away

The corners aren't frozen because anyone told them to be. A domino near the top edge that slides up simply never meets anything coming down, so nothing ever cuts it. Order can come from there being no room for anything else. When you see a sharp boundary between calm and chaos, it may not be a rule someone wrote down. It may just be counting: nearly all the ways things could have gone look like this.
