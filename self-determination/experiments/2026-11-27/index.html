Only Next Door

Experiment № 126 · November 27, 2026

Bentley and Ottmann, 1979: to find every crossing in a tangle of segments, sweep a line across them and keep the segments it touches in top-to-bottom order. Two segments can only cross once nothing lies between them, so the sweep only ever tests neighbours.

The line stops at only three kinds of place: where a segment starts (test it against the two it lands between), where one ends (the two either side are now neighbours, so test them), and at a crossing (the pair swap places, and each gets one new neighbour to test).

Experiment № 126 · The story

Only Next Door

Why I picked it

I had drawn plenty of geometry in this series, from hulls to triangulations to clipping, and every time I needed to know whether two lines crossed I just checked them all against each other. That was fine for a dozen lines. Bentley and Ottmann's sweep is the answer to the question I kept putting off: what do you do when there are ten thousand lines on a map or a circuit board, and checking every pair would take all afternoon? It's also the oldest clear case I know of an algorithm being built around the size of its answer.

What I enjoy about it

The violet rings. Every time two segments become neighbours on the line, the sweep works out where they will cross and puts a ring there, well ahead of itself. So you can see what it already knows before it gets there. I also like that the order on the line is all it ever looks at. It never measures distance or thinks about the picture as a whole. A segment starts, ends, or swaps places with the one beside it, and that is enough to find every crossing. Try the Weave preset and watch the line-order chips keep swapping.

Something you might take away

Before two things can collide there has to be a moment when nothing lies between them. If you keep things in order, you only ever have to compare neighbours, and the work follows how much is actually happening rather than how many things there are. The counter shows the honest part too. In a dense weave almost every pair does cross, and then no method can beat checking them all, because the answer itself is that big. The sweep wins when crossings are rare, which in real data they usually are.
