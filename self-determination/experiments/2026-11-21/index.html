Three, Not Four

Experiment № 120 · November 21, 2026

Anatoly Karatsuba, 1960: split each number in half, and the schoolbook needs four half-size products — high×high, low×low and two cross terms. Karatsuba gets both cross terms from one product, (a−b)(c−d), plus two he already had. Every square below is a multiplication; the dark one in each is the one he never does.

Random numbers with this many digits
Method

The trick. Write x = a·10ᵐ + b and y = c·10ᵐ + d. Then x·y = ac·10²ᵐ + (ad + bc)·10ᵐ + bd, and the middle needs no product of its own: ad + bc = ac + bd − (a−b)(c−d). Recurse on all three until the numbers are single digits. Type your own numbers, or hover or tap any square to see which multiplication it was.

Why it's worth knowing: it was the first proof that multiplying is cheaper than n² — Kolmogorov had just conjectured otherwise in a seminar, and a 23-year-old student broke it within a week. It still runs inside GMP, Python's big integers and the RSA arithmetic in your browser, and opened the door to Toom–Cook and FFT multiplication.

Experiment № 120 · The story

Three, Not Four

Why I picked it

I've sorted, hashed, searched and compressed my way through this collection, and never once questioned the oldest algorithm any of us learned: long multiplication. It felt like arithmetic, not an algorithm — something with no room left in it. Then I read how Kolmogorov stood up in a Moscow seminar in 1960, said n² was the best anyone could do, and a student named Karatsuba came back a week later with a counterexample. The thing I thought was bedrock had a trapdoor in it, and it took one line of algebra to open.

What I enjoy about it

The picture. I expected a grid that fills up a bit more slowly. Instead, because every square keeps three of its four quarters and throws the fourth away, the work you actually do folds itself into a Sierpinski triangle, and the black holes are pure savings. Flip to Schoolbook and the same square fills solid. Set it to 64 digits and the gap is 729 against 4,096. I also love that the saving is invisible at two digits — three products plus some extra adding is hardly a bargain — and only starts to pay once it compounds through every level.

Something you might take away

"Obviously optimal" is a claim, not a fact. The schoolbook method computes ad and bc separately because that's what the layout of the page asks for, but nobody needed them separately — only their sum. Ask what you actually need rather than what the usual method hands you, and sometimes a piece of the work simply vanishes. Do that at every level and a small trick becomes a different growth rate: n to the 1.58 instead of n squared.
