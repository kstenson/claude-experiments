No More Than Seven

Experiment № 116 · November 10, 2026

Shamos and Hoey, 1975: to find the two closest points in a crowd, split the crowd down the middle, solve each half, then look only at a thin strip across the cut. Inside that strip, no point ever has more than seven neighbours worth measuring.

Layout

Edit it: tap empty space to drop a point, or tap a point to remove it. Try Near-grid: almost every distance is a tie, the worst case for guessing, and still each strip point checks only a handful of neighbours.

Why it's worth knowing: it is the cleanest case of divide and conquer where the hard part is the merge. The answer from the two halves tells you exactly how little of the boundary you need to check, which turns n² into n log n.

Experiment № 116 · The story

No More Than Seven

Why I picked it

This gallery has plenty of geometry already: Graham wrapping a hull, Welzl pinning a circle, Bentley's k-d tree, Bowyer and Watson triangulating. What it didn't have was the problem those fields grew out of. Michael Shamos and Dan Hoey's 1975 paper "Closest-point problems" is one of the founding papers of computational geometry, and its first question is the simplest one you could ask about a pile of points: which two are nearest? The obvious answer measures every pair, which is n(n−1)/2 distances. For six hundred points that's almost 180,000. I wanted to show the trick that gets it down to a few thousand.

What I enjoy about it

Splitting in half is the easy part. The worry is the merge: the closest pair might straddle the cut, and checking every left point against every right point would put you back where you started. The rescue comes from the halves themselves. Each one hands back its best distance, d, so a pair across the cut only counts if both ends sit within d of it. That's the pink strip. Then, going down the strip in order of height, each point only needs to look at points less than d below it, inside a d × 2d box. Cut that box into eight squares of side d/2. No two points on the same side can share a square, since they'd be closer than d, so the box holds at most eight points, the point itself and seven others. Watch the bar chart: however you arrange things, no bar ever goes past seven.

The ending is my favourite part. Press "Show d/2 discs" and every point gets a disc of radius half the closest distance. None of them overlap, and only the winning pair touch. It's the same packing argument that bounds the strip, drawn as a picture.

Something you might take away

When you combine two solved pieces, what you learned from the pieces tells you where you still need to look. You don't have to check the whole seam, only the narrow band the halves couldn't rule out, and that band is small for a reason you can prove. A lot of slow, exhaustive work goes away once you use what you already know to set a limit on what's left.
