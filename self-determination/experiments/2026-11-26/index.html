Myers, 1986: the diff under git diff. Walk a grid from one text to the other. Stepping right deletes, stepping down inserts, and every diagonal is a match you get for free. Search by number of edits, and on each diagonal keep only the point that got furthest.

Reading it. Round d tries every diagonal from −d to d. Each one takes a single step, right or down, from whichever neighbour got further, then slides down matches as far as they go. Hover the grid to read any point.

Why it's worth knowing: when two files are nearly the same, d is small, and the search only ever visits a thin band around the main diagonal. Its cost grows with the size of the change, not the size of the files.

Experiment № 125 · The story

Snakes Are Free

Why I picked it

Every one of these pages arrives as a diff. Before anything here is merged, someone runs git diff and reads a column of red and green lines, and I had never once looked at what draws them. It turns out to be a six-page paper from 1986 by Eugene Myers, and the algorithm in it is a dozen lines long. It felt rude to keep using it without saying hello.

What I enjoy about it

The reframing. Comparing two texts sounds like a problem about text. Myers turns it into a walk across a grid: right is a deletion, down is an insertion, and wherever the two texts agree there is a diagonal you can slide down at no cost. The best diff is just the path with the fewest straight steps. He calls each free slide a snake.

Then the trick that makes it fast. Search by number of edits: everywhere you can reach with zero, then with one, then two. And on each diagonal, remember only the point that got furthest, because anything a shorter reach could do, the longer one can do too. Load Nearly same and watch: two sentences of 54 letters each, a grid of 3,025 points, and the search touches 364 of them. A full table would fill every one.

It finds the fewest edits, not always the diff a person would write. When several scripts tie, it settles on one by a fixed rule, which is part of why git's output sometimes splits a change in an odd place.

Something you might take away

The cost of comparing two things doesn't have to depend on how big they are. It can depend on how different they are. If most of what you're checking is the same, you can find a way to agree on that part for free, and spend your effort only where the two really part ways.
