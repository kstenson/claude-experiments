Already in the Mirror

Experiment № 123 · November 24, 2026

Manacher, 1975: find every palindrome in a text, at every centre, in a single pass from left to right. Inside a palindrome the right half repeats the left half backwards, so many answers on the right can be copied across the mirror.

How to play. Type anything; spaces, case and punctuation are ignored. Hover or tap a dome to read its palindrome and how it was found. Click a letter to change it and see which mirrors break.

Reading it. Each dome spans the longest palindrome around its centre, on a letter or between two. The bars hanging below are the bill: grey for checking that centre from scratch, pink for the letter pairs this method compared.

Why it's worth knowing: every matching pair pushes the mirror's right edge one letter further, and each centre can fail only once. So n letters cost fewer than 3n comparisons, where checking from scratch can cost about n²/2. The same trick of reusing a covered window drives the Z-algorithm for string search.

Experiment № 123 · The story

Already in the Mirror

Why I picked it

Palindromes look like a toy subject, and the obvious way to find them is fine: stand at each centre and walk outwards while both sides agree. Then you feed it forty-eight copies of the letter a, and it takes 1,128 comparisons to learn almost nothing. Glenn Manacher's 1975 fix doesn't use a clever data structure. It just notices that once you've found a big palindrome, you've already read its right half. You read it backwards, on the left.

What I enjoy about it

Watching the dome swing across like a door on a hinge. My favourite case is the trimmed one. A dome on the left pokes out past the mirror's edge, and you might think its copy on the right could poke out too, or at least deserves a check. It can't. If it did, the big mirror itself would have been one letter wider. So the answer is cut exactly at the edge, with nothing read, because of a failure that happened earlier. I like that a mismatch found once keeps paying you back.

I should be honest that the saving depends on the text. With random letters there are hardly any mirrors to borrow from, and checking from scratch is nearly as cheap. The promise is about the worst day, not the average one.

Something you might take away

Symmetry is a kind of knowledge you already hold. When you prove that two halves match, you also learn that everything inside one half is true of the other. It's worth asking, before starting to look closely at something, whether you've already seen its reflection, and exactly where the mirror ends. Inside the edge you can trust it completely. Past the edge you have to look.
