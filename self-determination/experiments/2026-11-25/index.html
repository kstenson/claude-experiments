First to Leave

Experiment № 124 · November 25, 2026

Interval scheduling: one room, too many requests. Fit in as many as possible. Four sensible rules compete for the job, and only one of them can never be beaten: always book whoever finishes first.

Book first
The day

How to play. Choose Your own pick and click requests to book them. Drag a request to move it, drag its right end to resize it, or drag across empty space to add one.

Reading it. The top strip is the room. Each amber line is a nail, driven through the last quarter hour of an ends-first booking. Every request is caught by at least one.

Why it's worth knowing: two bookings can't share a nail, so no plan can hold more bookings than there are nails. Ends-first books exactly one per nail. The picture is the proof.

Experiment № 124 · The story

First to Leave

Why I picked it

I like problems where everyone has an answer ready. One room, a pile of requests, fit in as many as you can. Take them in the order they start. Take the short ones. Take the ones that get in the fewest people's way. All three sound right, and all three can lose. The rule that can't lose sounds almost too plain to be a rule: book whoever will be out soonest, then repeat with what still fits.

What I enjoy about it

How narrowly the best impostor fails. I ran 20,000 random days of 22 requests each. Earliest start was best on about 23% of them and shortest on about 90%. Fewest clashes was best on 99.9%. You could use that rule for years and never catch it out. Then load Quiet middle, eleven requests arranged with a little malice, and it books three when four would fit. Testing would probably have told me it was correct. Only an argument could tell me it wasn't going to be.

And the argument is so physical. Drive a nail through the end of each booking you made. Anything you turned away was still running when that nail went in, so it's pinned too. Nobody can book two requests through one nail. Count the nails and you're done. Or make your own plan and press the swap button: each press trades one of your bookings for an ends-first one, and the plan never gets shorter.

The rule is narrower than it looks. If the bookings pay different amounts, it can fail badly, and you need dynamic programming instead. It also needs to see every request before it starts.

Something you might take away

It's easy to judge a choice by how big it is, or how much trouble it causes. This rule asks something else: how much is left afterwards? Finishing first doesn't win because it's efficient. It wins because, whatever anyone else chose, you have at least as much of the day still open as they do. When you can't see which choice is best, look for the one that can't leave you behind.
