As Far as It's Safe

Experiment № 119 · November 20, 2026

John C. Hart, 1996: sphere tracing. To find where a ray first touches a scene, ask one question at every stop — how far is the nearest surface, in any direction? — and leap exactly that far. Each leap is a circle that can't contain anything, so the ray can never jump through a wall.

Drag the eye to move it; drag anywhere else to aim it. Hover or tap the strip under the scene to spotlight one ray. The rule at every stop: measure the distance d to the nearest surface; if d is tiny you've hit it, otherwise step forward by d. Nothing can be closer than d, so the step is always safe.

Why it's worth knowing: it renders shapes you can only describe as a distance formula — fractals, blobs that melt into each other, infinite repeats — with no triangles at all. It's the engine behind most of Shadertoy and the demoscene, and the same distance fields sharpen game text, soften shadows and drive collision checks.

Experiment № 119 · The story

As Far as It's Safe

Why I picked it

I've drawn a lot of rays in this collection — a light finding its visibility polygon, a probe counting edge crossings — but every one of them already knew where the walls were. Sphere tracing is for scenes you can't list. All you have is a function that answers "how far to the nearest thing?", and from that one number you have to find the surface. When I first saw Hart's paper I assumed that couldn't be enough. It is, and the circles are the proof drawn right on the screen.

What I enjoy about it

The rhythm. A ray crossing open space takes two or three huge strides, then slows like someone feeling for a light switch as it approaches a surface — and never overshoots. I also like that the failure is honest: point a ray so it skims along the flat side of the corridor and the circles shrink to slivers, the step count explodes, and the ray goes pink. Sphere tracing is fast almost everywhere, and it shows you exactly where it isn't. Then turn up Melt and watch two pebbles fuse with a single min swapped for a soft one. No mesh could do that so cheaply.

Something you might take away

You don't have to inch forward when you know how much room you have. A guaranteed bound — "nothing is closer than this" — turns timid fixed steps into big confident leaps, and the tally shows it: often ten or twenty times fewer lookups than a careful fixed stride. The catch is in the word guaranteed. Overestimate the distance even a little and you'll walk straight through the wall. Be bold exactly as far as your evidence allows, and no further.
