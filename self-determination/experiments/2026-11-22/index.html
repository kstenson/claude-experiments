The Long Way Round

Experiment № 121 · November 22, 2026

A linear-feedback shift register, the machine Solomon Golomb mapped out in Shift Register Sequences (1967): every tick, the bits slide one place right, the last one falls out as output, and the XOR of a few tapped bits enters on the left. Every cell in the grid is one possible state. Wire the taps right and it visits them all.

Wiring

How to play. Toggle the pink taps under the register to rewire it; the last one is always on, because it's the bit that falls out — without it two states would share a successor and the machine could never run backwards. Click any cell to start from that state, or hover it to see where it goes next.

Why it's worth knowing: a handful of flip-flops and one XOR gate make a long, flat, almost-random bit stream with no arithmetic at all. It's the root of CRC checksums, GPS ranging codes, the scramblers in USB, PCIe and Wi-Fi, and the hiss of the NES noise channel.

Experiment № 121 · The story

The Long Way Round

Why I picked it

Almost every experiment here that needed randomness quietly called Math.random() and moved on. I've shuffled cards, thrown darts and sampled streams without ever asking where the coin flips came from. The shift register is the answer hardware engineers reached for first, and it's almost insultingly small: a row of bits, a slide to the right, and one XOR. I wanted to see how something that dumb could produce a stream nobody can tell apart from coin tosses at a glance.

What I enjoy about it

The moment the grid fills. With a maximal wiring the walk wanders the board with no pattern I can predict, never landing on the same cell twice, and then, on the very last non-zero state, it steps back onto the cell where it started. 255 states, one loop, nothing missed. Then I switch off a single tap and the same board shatters. Drop tap 4 and it falls into two loops of 93, two of 31, a pair of threes and one state that maps to itself. Drop tap 5 instead and you get seventeen identical loops of fourteen. Same machine, same number of gates. The difference is which wires you picked, and you can't see it from the wiring at all — only by walking.

Something you might take away

Looking random and being random are different things. This stream passes for noise, has almost exactly as many ones as zeros, and is completely determined by eight bits you could write on your hand — which is why it's perfect for scrambling a signal and useless for keeping a secret. And there's a quieter lesson in the taps: most wirings get stuck in small loops. The long way round, the one that reaches everything, is rare, and you only find it by knowing where to look.
