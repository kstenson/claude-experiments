Leave It Even

Experiment № 112 · November 6, 2026

Nim: on your turn, take as many stones as you like from one pile. You go first. The machine has never needed luck.

    The deal
    Last stone

    You 0Machine 0

    1. All even, and any move breaks it: changing one pile changes at least one size, so that count turns odd.
    2. Anything odd, and one move mends it: find a pile holding the largest odd size and rebuild it. Dropping that block pays for the rest.
    3. The empty table is all even. So whoever keeps leaving even takes the last stone.

    Experiment № 112 · The story

    Leave It Even

    Why I picked it

    Most of these pages ask you to watch. This one had to let you lose. Charles Bouton's 1901 paper in the Annals of Mathematics gave Nim its name and its complete solution, which makes it one of the first games anyone ever solved outright. It then became one of the first games a machine played in public: Edward Condon's Nimatron at the 1940 New York World's Fair, and Ferranti's Nimrod at the Festival of Britain in 1951. No. 071 here plays by searching a tree of replies. Nim needs no search at all, and I wanted to put that difference on a table where you could feel it.

    What I enjoy about it

    The moment I like most is the switch. You play a few rounds against something that seems to see everything, then you press one button and the same stones slide apart into blocks of eight, four, two and one, and the opponent turns out to be counting. Its whole strategy is a single exclusive-or, shorter than any sentence on this page that describes it. I also like the Rigged deal, which is a position where the best thing you can do is lose slowly, and last-stone-loses, where Bouton's rule holds right up to the end and flips only when one big pile remains. Before publishing I checked the rule against a plain brute-force search of the game, in both versions, because a page that claims to be unbeatable ought to be.

    Something you might take away

    A winning strategy doesn't have to be a plan. The machine never looks ahead. It keeps one property true after each of its moves, and the proof only needs three facts: the end state has the property, your opponent can't keep it, and you can always bring it back. That shape, an invariant you keep restoring, is how you reason about loops, locks and ledgers as well as games. And Nim turned out to be bigger than itself: in the 1930s Sprague and Grundy showed that every impartial game, however strange its rules, behaves like a Nim pile of some size.
