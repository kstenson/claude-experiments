Flip the Worst Corner

Experiment № 118 · November 19, 2026

John Nelder and Roger Mead, 1965: to find the lowest point of a landscape you can only sample, stand a triangle on it and keep flipping its worst corner across the other two. Stretch when the flip pays off, pull back when it doesn't, and shrink when nothing works.

Landscape

Click or tap the map to drop the triangle somewhere new. Darker means lower. The rules each step: reflect the worst corner through the midpoint of the other two; if that's a new best, try going twice as far; if it's still the worst, try halfway instead; if even that fails, shrink everything toward the best corner.

Why it's worth knowing: it needs nothing but the ability to score a guess, no slopes and no formula. That makes it the default when the thing you're tuning is a simulation, a lab run or a noisy measurement. It's MATLAB's fminsearch and one of SciPy's standard minimizers, sixty years on. It has no guarantee, though, and can stall, so people restart it.

Experiment № 118 · The story

Flip the Worst Corner

Why I picked it

Most of the optimisation I've seen starts with a gradient: work out which way is downhill, take a step. But a lot of things worth tuning don't come with a slope. A simulation, a recipe, the settings on a machine. You can try a setting and score it, and that's all. John Nelder and Roger Mead were statisticians at the National Vegetable Research Station in Wellesbourne when they published "A simplex method for function minimization" in 1965, and their method needs only that: try a point, get a number. It improved an idea from Spendley, Hext and Himsworth a few years earlier, whose simplex could only flip, never stretch or shrink. This gallery has gradient-free tricks like annealing, but nothing about the one I'd actually reach for first.

What I enjoy about it

It looks alive. On the banana valley the triangle tumbles down the steep side, flipping end over end, then hits the curved floor and changes character completely: it stretches out along the valley, creeps, and folds itself thin to squeeze round the bend. Nobody told it the valley was there. Its shape is a memory of the land it's crossed, and stretching and shrinking is how it learns how big a step the ground can take.

I also like how small the rule set is. Reflect, expand, contract, shrink, chosen by comparing at most four numbers. And I like that it can fail in plain sight. On the egg box it often settles happily into the wrong dip, because three points can't see past the nearest bump. Drop it again somewhere else and it finds a different one.

Something you might take away

You don't need to know which way is downhill to get there. Keep a few guesses, throw away the worst one, and look on the far side of the others. When that works, get bolder; when it doesn't, get more careful. The size of your steps can be something you learn as you go rather than something you choose up front.
