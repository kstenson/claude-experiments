Experiment № 113 · The story

Without Multiplying

Why I picked it

I write Math.sin as if it were free, the way you flick a light switch without thinking about the power station. So I wanted to look behind one. Jack Volder needed trigonometry for the navigation computer of a bomber in 1956, on hardware where a multiplier was expensive and slow, and his answer sat underneath pocket calculators for decades. It still gets built into chips today. After a run of games and graphs, I liked the idea of a piece about something so humble it has disappeared.

What I enjoy about it

The moment the trick stops being a trick. Rotating properly needs a cosine and a sine, which are the very things you're trying to compute. Volder throws the cosine away and keeps the tangent, then chooses only angles whose tangent is one half, one quarter, one eighth. Now a turn is just "push the tip sideways by a shifted copy of itself". Watch the trail: it's straight, at right angles to the needle, and that's why the needle gets longer.

That looks like a defect, and I love that it isn't. Each stretch depends only on the size of the turn, never on its direction, so as long as no turn is skipped the total is the same for every angle: 1.6468. Press Skip while steering and you'll break that. Keep every turn, and the error is known before you start, so you begin short by exactly that much and land on the circle.

Something you might take away

A fault you can't remove may still be one you can make constant, and a constant fault can be paid for in advance. It's also a good picture of how to search with fixed moves: each one is small enough that the rest can always undo it, so you never need to stop and think, you only need to know which side you're on. The second mode is the same loop with one line changed, steering y to zero instead of z, and it gives you an arctangent and a square root for nothing.
