Experiment № 127 · November 28, 2026

Dantzig, 1947: to find the best point in a region cut out by straight lines, never search the inside. The best is always at a corner, so start at one and walk along any edge that goes uphill, until no edge does. The table underneath is the same walk written as arithmetic.

Experiment № 127 · The story

Always a Corner

Why I picked it

I had done plenty of searching in this series: mazes, game trees, a triangle feeling its way down a landscape. All of them were hunting through a space that could hide its best point anywhere. Linear programming is the case where it can't, and the simplex method has quietly run more of the world than almost anything else here. Dantzig built it in 1947 to plan US Air Force logistics, and some descendant of it is still deciding airline crews, refinery blends and shipping routes this morning. It felt wrong to keep it out.

What I enjoy about it

That the picture and the table are the same thing, and neither looks like the other. On the board a dot slides along an edge. In the table a column lights up, a ratio wins, and a whole row is divided and subtracted from everything else. I like setting the uphill slider and guessing the route before pressing play. The Round preset is the patient one: the dot can only ever step to a neighbouring corner, so it has to crawl halfway round the rim, one short edge at a time, and switching rules sends it round the other side. Degenerate is my favourite. With the biggest-gain rule, the very first pivot has length zero: the table changes, the dot doesn't move, and for one step the arithmetic and the geometry come apart.

Something you might take away

Sometimes the clever move is noticing where an answer can't be. Once you see that a straight-line objective over a straight-edged region peaks at a corner, an infinite search becomes a finite walk, and convexity tells you that a corner with no better neighbour is the best of all. The catch is honest too. In 1972 Klee and Minty built squashed cubes where the walk visits every single corner, so in theory simplex can be exponentially slow. In practice it almost never is, and it took until 2001 for Spielman and Teng to explain why: those bad cases fall apart if you nudge them even slightly.
